## 1. The terms you asked about

**Aira** — a paid human-assistance service: a blind user opens an app and a
trained sighted agent sees their camera feed and talks them through anything
(reading mail, navigating an airport). It works extremely well and costs
**~$1.30–1.50 per minute** ($26/mo for 20 minutes). "Rationed emergency
resource" means users save it for crises because of the price — which is the
opportunity: anything we automate for free replaces rationed human minutes.

**Microsoft Soundscape** — the most-loved blind-navigation app ever shipped
(Microsoft killed it in 2023, then open-sourced it — we can read all its
design decisions in code, and we've adopted several: the 60 s no-repeat rule,
silence as the default state, volume used ONLY to signal confidence). Its
core feature was the **audio beacon**: a gentle repeating sound that seems to
come *from* your destination's direction through your headphones, so you walk
toward the sound instead of listening to "turn left in 20 meters."

**Entrance / doorway / last-meter / terminal guidance** — four names for one
problem. GPS gets a blind person to "you have arrived" — which means
somewhere within ~10 m of a building. Now they face 40 m of storefront: wall,
window, bike rack, planter, and one actual door, and the cane can't tell
which is which without trailing the whole facade. **"Last meter"** = that
final gap GPS abandons. **"Terminal guidance"** = our name for the fix:
the camera spots the door, the ToF confirms it's a real opening (depth
behind it) and gives exact range, and audio/haptics steer you to it — like
a plane landing on instruments. Same mechanic works for elevators, counters,
bus poles, seats.

**Queue detection and progress** — standing in line is genuinely hard blind:
where does the line end? Is it moving? Am I leaving a gap? Am I next? A
proven system (LineChaser, CHI 2021 — built on exactly our sensor combo,
camera + depth) finds the end of the line, tells you when the person ahead
moves ("line moved, step forward about two steps"), and says when you're at
the front. "Progress" = those move-up notifications.

**Empty seat finding** — walking into a waiting room / bus / lecture hall
and finding an unoccupied chair without touching strangers or asking. Camera
finds chairs, depth says which are empty (a person sitting there = a closer
return), guidance steers you. Blind users specifically listed this as a task
researchers ignore.

**The "social gap"** — the research shorthand for the interpersonal stuff
vision does at a party: who's in the room, where your friend is, whether
someone's approaching to talk, whether you're facing the person speaking.
Users called it their biggest pain ("I've lost count of the times I've ended
up talking to an empty chair"). The research verdict was that our sensors
serve it poorly EXCEPT empty-seat finding — but see your privacy decision
below, which reopens parts of it.

**SKU-level unwinnable** — SKU = one exact product ("Heinz ketchup 500 ml
squeeze bottle"). Recognizing *specific products* on shelves means keeping up
with ~30,000 NEW products per year, forever — a database war that even
grocery chains struggle with. **Aisle-level** ("cereal aisle", "dairy
section") needs only signs and categories — winnable. Reading a label
**in your hand** is also winnable (it's OCR, one product, close up) — and
you've now flagged that as a feature to add. ✅

**Person following ("walk with me")** — lock onto a chosen person (a friend,
a guide walking ahead) and give continuous bearing/distance so you can
follow them through a crowd without holding their arm — hands free for the
cane. Technically: ByteTrack already gives us per-person IDs; following = a
beacon on one tracked ID. The research called demand "thin" but the
precedent systems (BBeep, LineChaser) prove the tracking works. You asked to
hear more → queued for the new research pass.

**Standalone pedestrian detection** — announcing every person near you
("person left, person right, person ahead") as its own feature. Users
rejected THAT ("people see you coming and move; the dog walks around them").
It's noise. But people as *inputs* to other features — queue tracking,
person following, collision-course warnings — stay. That's the distinction.

**BuzzClip** (if you hit it in the reading) — a small clip-on ultrasonic
obstacle beeper. In the gold-standard trial it performed WORSE than a bare
cane and users rated it most frustrating: undifferentiated beeps = noise.
The cautionary tale behind our "informative or silent" rule.

## 2. Your decisions, logged (I build to these)

1. **Glass/touchscreen strategy: add a CV material detector** (glass/mirror
   segmentation — GDNet-B / RGB-D glass-detection literature already in our
   research). Lean on camera where ToF is blind. → added to IDEA-BANK as ✅.
2. **Label-reading-in-hand: add** as a feature candidate. ✅
3. **Compute is not a constraint.** Build for the future; use the GPU; a
   Jetson-Orin-class device is the eventual commercial target. Real-time on
   this laptop is the only bar. → unblocks heavier models (better seg,
   monocular depth, material detection).
4. **Head-height protection via geometry**: sensors sit ABOVE the head on the
   helmet; rule = keep **10–15 cm of clearance above the TOP ToF ray** to
   any overhead obstacle and the head provably can't hit anything. (Top ray
   of the 22.5°-down group ≈ horizontal, so this works as stated; sensors
   can tilt up ~1° on the ball mount if margin is thin.) → implement as an
   explicit "head-clearance" check on the top zone rows.
5. **Privacy/regulatory concerns: NOT a constraint right now.** Maximize
   research capability and impressiveness; features can be pulled back
   later. Facial recognition: *considering* — you lean "not necessarily
   useful" but it's not privacy-blocked anymore. (Noted: the research docs
   record the community's objections for whenever this goes public.)
6. **Terrain detection: do NOT cross out** — rocky/uneven terrain warning may
   be useful. Reopened for research (the earlier rejection was for flat-city
   walking where feet sense it).
7. **Currency detection: reconsider** — reopened.
8. **The cane is used alongside the device.** (Matches every research
   verdict; stays in all claims language.)
9. **Bone conduction: buy one** — affordable, on Amazon, fast shipping, easy
   to integrate (standard Bluetooth/3.5 mm). → buying-guide research queued.
10. **Crosswalk alignment: build-intent confirmed** → deep research queued.
11. **More research ordered** (agents launching): doorway/entrance guidance
    done RIGHT (how to make it as good as possible), head-height obstacle
    detection best practice, bus/route-number + public transport assistance
    generally **including getting into cars/rideshares**, dropped-object
    finding feasibility (can our resolution see small objects on the floor —
    math + evidence), empty-seat state of the art, person-following.

## 3. Corrections/notes on your geometry point

One nuance on "ToFs are currently 0°": the pair is group-tilted 22.5° DOWN
(CAD-verified), so the grid spans from ~0° (top row edge ≈ horizontal) to
~45° down. Your safety rule still holds — the TOP ray is the head-clearance
ray — but the margin math must use the top-row zone rays specifically, and
the ball mount means the leveling step (voice-guided, key `l`) is what
guarantees the top ray really is horizontal on any given day.
