# 1. THE CAMERA MOUNT BASEPLATE

A **wedge**: flat bottom (mounts to the camera plate / ball head), **22.5° top face**.

## Why a 22.5° wedge is the right shape

**It is the physical realisation of the rigid-group construction.** Build the sensor
group level, then tilt the lot 22.5° as one rigid rotation — the wedge *is* step 3.
That keeps the zero seam intact by construction rather than by re-aiming anything.

⚠️ **22.5° is correct ONLY because the whole sensor group sits on it.** If a camera
board were to lie flat on that face instead, the face would need to be **67.5° from
horizontal** — a board lying on a surface looks *perpendicular* to it. This is the same
22.5-vs-67.5 trap that has caught us repeatedly.

| What mounts on top | Top face angle |
|---|---|
| the whole sensor group, built level | **22.5° from the bottom** ✅ |
| a camera board lying flat on it | 67.5° from horizontal |

## Verification targets
- bottom face vs Top Plane → **0.00°**
- top face vs Top Plane → **22.50°**
- top face vs bottom face → **22.50°**

## ⚠️ A 22.5° wedge climbs fast

```
rise = tan(22.5°) = 0.414 mm of height per mm of length
```

| Wedge length | Rise |
|---|---|
| 20 mm | 8.3 mm |
| 40 mm | **16.6 mm** |
| 60 mm | 24.9 mm |

**But screw length only limits thickness AT THE SCREW HOLES**, not across the whole
plate. Put the holes toward the thin end and the plate can be as thick as it likes
further along. **Position the holes before fixing the length.**

## How to model it
Sketch the profile **from the side** and extrude — one sketch, one feature.

1. Sketch on the plane that shows the wedge **edge-on**; **Ctrl+8** to face it
2. Draw a closed 4-sided profile: bottom horizontal line · short vertical at the
   **thin** end · sloped line to the thick end · vertical to close
3. **Smart Dimension between the sloped line and the bottom line → 22.5°**
4. **Extruded Boss/Base, Mid Plane**, set the width

**Make it a trapezoid, not a triangle** — give the thin end 3–4 mm of real material. A
knife edge prints badly and has no strength.

# 2. FASTENER STACK — M5

## Hardware [CALIPER]
| | |
|---|---|
| Screw | **M5 × 20 mm** (length excludes the head) |
| Washer | **1.4 mm** |
| Nut | **3.8 mm** ⚠️ DIN 934 M5 is 4.0 — measure across flats too |
| Camera baseplate (existing part) | **3.6 mm** |
| Screw spacing | **20.5 mm** |
| Count | **2 screws** |

## Available thickness

```
20.0 screw − 3.6 camera plate − 1.4 washer − 3.8 nut = 11.2 mm max
```

| Setup | Max | **Design to** |
|---|---|---|
| 3.6 plate + 1 washer | 11.2 mm | **10.0 mm** |
| 3.6 plate + 2 washers | 9.8 mm | **8.5 mm** |
| No 3.6 plate + 2 washers | 13.4 mm | **12.0 mm** |
| **With a nut trap** (nut recessed, see below) | **15.0 mm** | 13.5 mm |

**Leave ~1 mm of margin.** At the exact maximum the screw tip finishes flush with the
outer nut face — full engagement but zero slack, and any part printing a few tenths
thick means the nut won't fully thread on. Seeing 1–2 threads past the nut is also how
you confirm by eye that it's properly made up.

# 3. ⚠️ THE REAL RISK ON THIS JOINT IS SLIP, NOT STRENGTH

Load check: camera assembly ~25 g at ~40 mm from the mount, 2 g of helmet bob →
**~0.04 N·m**. Two M5s at 20.5 mm resist orders of magnitude more. **Strength is not
the constraint.**

**A bolted joint through clearance holes is held in position by FRICTION, not by the
screws touching the hole walls.** If friction is ever overcome, the plate shifts by
however much clearance was given, and stops. M5 through a standard Ø5.5 hole means
**up to 0.5 mm of movement** — precisely the shift that silently invalidates the
Stage 3 extrinsics.

**Three defences, in order of value:**

1. **Washers under head AND nut.** They let real clamping force develop without the
   head crushing into plastic. Without them the plastic yields, preload drops, friction
   goes with it. **Plastic creep under a bare screw head is the standard way this joint
   goes loose — weeks later, long after calibration.**
2. **Close-fit holes — Ø5.2 mm, not 5.5.** Halves the worst-case slip. Test-print
   first; FDM holes come out undersized.
3. **A locating boss/pin** between the screws takes shear off the friction joint
   entirely. At 20.5 mm spacing there's room.

**Also:** keep ≥3 mm of material around each hole, and space the screws as far apart as
the plate allows — resistance to rocking scales with separation, and the line between
them should cross the axis you most expect to rock (vertical bob, on a helmet).

# 4. M5 NUT TRAP

**Hole Wizard CANNOT make hex pockets** — it does round features only (counterbore,
countersink, tap). The newer **Slot** tab makes round-ended slots, not hexagons.

**Use an Extruded Cut with a polygon sketch:**

1. Sketch on the face the nut drops into
2. **Tools → Sketch Entities → Polygon**, **6 sides**
3. Choose **Inscribed circle** — that is the **across-flats** dimension, which is how
   nuts are specified
4. **Extruded Cut, Blind**

| | Across flats | Inscribed Ø to sketch | Nut thickness | Pocket depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **M5** (DIN 934) | 8.0 mm | **8.2 mm** | 4.0 mm | **4.2 mm** |
| ours (3.8 mm nut) | measure it | ~8.2 mm | 3.8 mm | **4.0 mm** |
| M4 (DIN 934) | 7.0 mm | 7.2 mm | 3.2 mm | 3.4 mm |

**Still cut the screw clearance hole** through the middle — **Ø5.2 mm** for M5. The
pocket only holds the nut.

**A nut trap buys back 3.8 mm of plate thickness** — the nut is recessed *into* the
plate instead of stacked below it, so it stops consuming screw length. Max plate goes
**11.2 → 15.0 mm**. Useful given how fast the wedge climbs.

**Two more benefits:** no spanner needed on the far side, and the pocket floor spreads
load over far more area than a washer — directly addressing the creep risk.
**Still put a washer under the head**, which now bears on plastic.

**Practical:**
- **Orient the flats deliberately** — the nut can't rotate once seated; pick an
  orientation that keeps pocket walls thick
- **Plan how the nut gets in** — a pocket on a face is easy; one *inside* the part
  needs a side access slot, or drop it in mid-print with a printer pause
- A pocket on a **downward** face prints cleanly; on an **upward** face the material
  above bridges — fine at this size, and 0.2 mm oversize helps

# 5. ⚠️ SOLIDWORKS — NEW TRAPS FOUND

## Orphaned lock files stop SOLIDWORKS opening files

**Happened twice.** If SOLIDWORKS crashes or is killed, it leaves a `~$<filename>` per
open document. The next launch sees them, believes the files are open elsewhere, and
**refuses / opens read-only / appears to hang**.

**Fix:**
1. Confirm **no `SLDWORKS.exe`** is running (Task Manager → Details)
2. Delete every `~$*` file in the folder
3. Reopen

On 2026-08-07 four orphans from a session that died at 11:13 the previous day were
blocking the live assembly entirely. Deleting them fixed it instantly.

## Over-constrained mates: Width + Coincident conflict

**Two Width mates plus a face-to-face Coincident breaks.** Width mates don't only
centre — depending on the selections they can lock **rotation** too. By the time the
Coincident is added, orientation is already fixed at whatever the Widths landed on, and
the two demands conflict.

**Correct order: Coincident FIRST (sets orientation), Width mates SECOND (centring
only).** Give the orienting job to the mate meant to have it.

If a Coincident alone still lands wrong, click **Mate Alignment** flip
(aligned ↔ anti-aligned) — the usual cause of a face-to-face mate sitting backwards.

## "It looks angled" is usually not a mate problem

The assembly's world axes are locked to the **left ToF sensor**, so **nothing sits
level on screen**. Don't diagnose from the render.

**Measure the face against the Top Plane:** 0.00° means it genuinely is flat and only
*looks* tilted because everything is.

## Appearances — per-feature transparency

Appearance precedence: **Face > Feature > Body > Part > Component.** Left overrides
right, so a feature-level appearance survives a later part-level recolour (and a
part-level change won't clear it — use **Appearance → Remove Appearance**).

**To make one extrude transparent:** right-click the **feature** in the tree → beach-ball
icon → pick the level showing the **feature name** → Transparency slider.

**Faster: the Display Pane** — the **»** at the top-right of the FeatureManager slides
out per-item columns for hide/show, display mode, appearance and transparency. Shows at
a glance what's already set.

⚠️ A feature-level appearance lives in the **part file**, so it appears in every
assembly using that part. For assembly-local colour, apply at **component** level.

## Surface Finish does nothing without RealView

The **Surface Finish** tab (Treadplate etc.) is a **render-only** effect. It needs
**RealView Graphics** (`View → Display`) — which requires a certified GPU and driver
and is **greyed out on most laptops** — or a PhotoView 360 render.

It changes nothing geometric and never appears in the standard shaded view. For a
visible change that always works, use the **Color/Image** tab. For real print texture,
this is the wrong tool entirely — that's modelled geometry or a slicer setting.
